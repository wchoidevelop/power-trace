Power Trace

A mobile application that maps power structures inside any organization — a company, a club, a congregation — using organizational psychology research and live federal labor-market data.
Wonjun Choi
wchoici2027@gmail.com

Conflict inside an organization is usually explained as a personality problem. More often it is a structural one — a question of who holds which kind of leverage, and who has to route through whom. Power Trace asks a user to describe their organization in plain language, scores every role across the six bases of social power established by French and Raven (1959), audits the resulting map for the biases inherent in one person having written all of it, and reads the user's situation against that map. For paid workplaces it adds current U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data on the user's industry; for unpaid organizations it says so and reasons without it.

The problem it addresses

People in organizational conflicts routinely misjudge their own leverage. They read a manager's formal authority as total, miss the colleague whose control over information decides what leadership ever hears, and assume that leaving is an option when their sector's hiring has effectively frozen. Each of those is a known, well-studied phenomenon with decades of research behind it — but that research sits in academic literature, not in the hands of the person at 9 p.m. deciding whether to send the email.

The gap is not information; it is application. Power Trace exists to apply established frameworks to one specific situation, and to be honest about the limits of doing so. It began as an attempt to make sense of one real organization, where the formal structure explained almost nothing about who actually decided things.

How it works

Six stages. Each narrows the uncertainty left by the last, and the map is audited before anything is built on it.

1 Describe The user describes their organization, the roles in it, and their own position, in ordinary language.
2 Score A language model scores every role 0–10 on each of the six power bases — legitimate, reward, coercive, expert, referent, informational — and labels each score as drawn from what the description stated, what it implied, or what had to be assumed.
3 Clarify A second pass reads the whole map and writes targeted questions about the assumed scores that would actually change the picture — never about scores the description already settled.
4 Audit The answers are folded in, and a bias pass checks the account for self-minimization, antagonist inflation, victim framing, and four other documented distortions. Every flag must quote the user's own words and carry the strongest argument that the flag itself is wrong.
5 Contextualize For paid roles, the user's position is matched to federal occupation and industry classifications by semantic search, then joined to live BLS wage, union-coverage, and labor-turnover data for that sector. Skipped for unpaid organizations, which have no entry in those classifications.
6 Diagnose The situation is read against the finished map and the market data, producing a structural diagnosis, the two or three research frameworks that actually fit, and a prediction of which response — exit, voice, loyalty, or neglect — the configuration pushes toward, with a better alternative.

Commercial and non-commercial organizations

French and Raven describe social power, not employment. The same six bases run a company, a school club, a church, a volunteer board, or an online community with moderators, so the power map applies to all of them unchanged. Only the labor-market stage is employment-specific, and it is skipped rather than approximated when it does not apply.

The distinction is analytically real, not just a matter of coverage. With no salary to grant and no job to threaten, reward and coercive power shrink in an unpaid organization and influence concentrates in expertise, relationships, and control of information. What makes leaving costly changes with it: in a job that is an economic question the BLS data can speak to, and in a volunteer group it is a social one, so the analysis states which it is reasoning about rather than importing employment logic where it does not belong.

Research foundation

The analysis draws only from a fixed whitelist of established findings, and citations are attached in code from a reference table rather than generated — a language model asked to produce a citation will eventually invent one.

French & Raven (1959); Raven (2004) — the six bases of social power. The scoring model itself: each base rated on potential influence rather than on whether it has been used.

Raven, Schwarzwald & Koslowsky (1998) — soft versus harsh influence. Harsh bases buy compliance at a cost to satisfaction; the map shows which kind a position runs on.

Hirschman (1970); Rusbult (1988) — exit, voice, loyalty, neglect. The four responses to dissatisfaction, and the app's central prediction.

Edmondson (1999) — psychological safety. Whether raising a problem is survivable, which usually decides between voice and silence.

Adams (1965); Greenberg (1987) — equity theory and organizational justice. Perceived fairness of outcomes, and of the process that produced them.

Ross (1977); Tajfel & Turner (1979) — fundamental attribution error and social identity. Why an adversary's behavior reads as character rather than circumstance.

Milgram (1963); Asch (1951); Darley & Latané (1968); Janis (1972) — obedience, conformity, diffused responsibility, groupthink. Why bystanders stay quiet.

Tepper (2000); Cortina et al. (2001) — abusive supervision and workplace incivility, for the low-grade patterns that are hard to name.

Hofstede (1980); Festinger (1957); Goffman (1959) — power distance, cognitive dissonance, impression management.

Labor-market integration

The app pulls directly from two Bureau of Labor Statistics programs: the Current Population Survey, for median weekly earnings by sex and union coverage in the user's industry and occupation, and the Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, for hires, quits, layoffs, and openings in their sector.

A raw turnover rate is meaningless across industries — quits run near 4–5% a month in leisure and hospitality and near 2% in information — so every rate is positioned before it is shown or sent to the model: a z-score against that same sector's 2015–2019 pre-pandemic mean, a percentile against nineteen years of its own history, and three- and twelve-month changes. The app then classifies the sector as tight, frozen, contracting, reallocating, or balanced, a joint read of hiring and separations. That distinction is what makes the advice honest: in a frozen market, openings can look healthy while almost nobody actually moves, so treating exit as leverage would be wrong.

Technical architecture

Mobile client

React Native 0.81 on Expo 54, TypeScript, file-based routing with Expo Router. Runs on iOS, Android, and web from one codebase. Custom theming with light and dark modes; animation via Reanimated. Analyses are saved locally on the device, and can be reopened and reviewed step by step.

API server

Next.js 16 route handlers on React 19, TypeScript throughout. MongoDB Atlas provides vector search over occupation definitions for role classification. The OpenAI Responses API is used with Zod-derived structured-output schemas, so every model response is validated against a type before it is trusted.

Engineering decisions

Arithmetic belongs in code, not in the model

The composite power index is computed from the six scores by a pure function with fixed weights. A model asked to produce a weighted average will produce a plausible one, and plausible is not the same as correct.

Provenance instead of self-reported confidence

Each score is labelled stated, implied, or assumed. That is a fact about the input text, checkable by re-reading it — unlike a model's own confidence estimate, which is introspection and poorly calibrated. The questionnaire stage then works from provenance rather than from where a score happens to sit on the scale, since a confident 5 is a real mid-range finding and an assumed 9 is still an assumption.

Every bias flag carries the case against itself

The audit must quote the user's own words and state the strongest reading in which the flag is wrong and the account is simply accurate. Both are shown. A tool that tells someone their experience is a distortion, without argument, would do real harm — particularly since retaliation and targeting are genuine workplace phenomena, not only perceptions.

"No answer" is an answer

Asked to classify a swimming club against federal occupation codes, the classifier correctly returns nothing — no published classification describes unpaid work. The first version treated that as a failure and showed an error, because it then requested zero data series and the server rejected the empty request. It now reports plainly that no labor data applies and continues without it. Distinguishing "this went wrong" from "there is nothing here" is most of what makes the non-commercial case work.

Failure handling matched to cost

Every network call can fail and be retried, but the retry policy differs by what is being retried. Free, idempotent data pulls retry automatically with exponential backoff. Reasoning calls that run over a minute never retry on their own: aborting the request does not cancel the work on the server, so an automatic retry would run a second analysis alongside the first and bill for both. There, retrying is the user's decision.

Privacy and ethics

Users describing a workplace conflict are handling sensitive material, often about colleagues who never agreed to any of it. The design reflects that. There are no accounts and no personal details are requested. Nothing a user types is stored on the server — text exists only for the seconds it takes to process a request. Saved analyses live on the user's own device and can be deleted there. The app contains no analytics, tracking, or advertising software of any kind.

The terms of service state plainly that this is educational and is not legal, HR, or professional advice, and forbid using it to evaluate employees or build a case against a specific person. Both documents are served from the same source the app reads, so a correction reaches users without waiting on an app release.

Status

The full six-stage pipeline is implemented and working end to end, with the client, API server, labor-data integration, local persistence, and legal documents complete, for both commercial and non-commercial organizations. The project is a working prototype in active development, not yet publicly released. Planned next steps are a public deployment, an accessibility pass, and validating the scoring against described cases where the power structure is already known.
